## The Story

After tasting the boom and bust of semiconductors, I kept in mind something Bill Gross once said: the music only stops when major central banks — the Fed, the BOJ — push their yields to a suffocating level, whether through inflation or overly flush liquidity. Until then, capital *must* go looking for the next big trade idea. The question was: where?

Geopolitical alpha. I first read the phrase in an Amazon book back in 2020 — never finished it, to be honest — but the core idea stuck: straits, whether Hormuz or Suez, have always been bottlenecks worth watching. COVID had already shown how shipping companies steamrolled downstream exporters, sending freight rates sky-high and collecting the windfall.

That thought resurfaced around the same time as the first sign of war at the end of March. And that's when I saw BWET — an exchange-traded partnership. Back then I had zero idea what it actually did. Opening Excel, I saw rows of FFA codes that looked arcane: cryptic acronyms, routes, destination ports, contract maturities. It wasn't until September, with AI's help, that I finally understood what it was really earning on: a massive backwardation, driven by a series of binary geopolitical events that kept surprising the market.

Everyone expected Trump to TACO — to back down and let things normalize. This time it just kept dragging on. Freight rates kept getting repriced every time BWET rolled its positions. And the FFA itself had effectively become a new form of insurance, because traditional maritime insurers had given up underwriting the route.

I spotted it. I never pursued it — for various reasons, mostly because I was too engrossed in my explosive semiconductor trades, without realizing that a crowded trade is a soured trade. The lesson that stays with me: venturing into narrower avenues often offers far better asymmetric upside.

## Rationale — Why This Happened

- **Chokepoints concentrate risk into prices.** When a strait carrying a meaningful share of global trade flow becomes dangerous, the cost of moving goods through it doesn't rise linearly — it reprices violently, because demand for shipping is inelastic in the short run. Exporters still have to ship; the freight market extracts the maximum toll.
- **Backwardation is the market paying you to carry risk today.** When spot and near-month freight futures trade far above later maturities, a rolling long position collects positive roll yield — as long as the crisis persists. Each "binary event" (attack, closure, failed negotiation) pushes the curve back out, and the repricing repeats. That was the engine under BWET's returns.
- **When insurers exit, derivatives become the insurance market.** Once traditional maritime underwriters refused the route, hedging demand migrated to the FFA market itself, adding a structural bid to forward freight. Understanding *who the marginal buyer of a contract is* often explains more than any supply/demand model.
- **Consensus geopolitical expectations are a tradeable mispricing.** "Everyone expected a TACO" means positioning was built on de-escalation. When the base case failed, the unwind of that consensus — not the conflict itself — was where the convex payoff lived.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Capital always hunts the next bottleneck.** When one crowded trade matures, ask where the next physical or informational chokepoint is: straits, ports, pipelines, spectrum, fabs, grid capacity. Bottlenecks are where inelastic demand meets constrained supply — the recipe for asymmetric pricing.
2. **Understand the instrument before admiring the returns.** I saw BWET early but couldn't decode FFAs, routes, and roll mechanics until months later. An opportunity you can't explain is an opportunity you can't size — build the learning loop *before* you need it.
3. **Don't let a winning trade blind you to the next one.** My semiconductor positions were performing, so I had no bandwidth for a stranger, narrower idea. Attention is a portfolio too; diversify it deliberately, especially when your current book feels euphoric.
4. **Consensus geopolitical outcomes are fragile.** When "everyone" prices a resolution (TACO), the cost of being wrong is low and the payoff for being right is high. Fade consensus carefully, but respect how crowded the consensus side is.
5. **Narrow avenues reward early, honest research.** Precisely because few people understand an instrument, the mispricing persists longer. The entry ticket is effort, not capital.

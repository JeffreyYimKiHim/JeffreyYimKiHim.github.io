## The Story

Bitcoin was once a narrow avenue too — ten years ago, almost nobody understood what it was. What happened in the memory-chip complex over the past year resembled that same frantic rally, and underneath both moves sat similar drivers.

A market's pricing mechanism typically *malfunctions* when it faces a genuinely new situation or a new asset that very few people understand upfront. It's almost like the pump-and-dump dynamics in crypto IPOs and NFTs: hopeful people are more willing to hold at the early stage, and those with the information advantage are still busy distributing — or propping up the market at whatever cost they can bear.

It's only after major corrections take the fun out of it that people begin to look more closely, identify the cyclicality baked into the asset, and finally give it a more honest price tag.

Look at Samsung Electronics: despite being a major HBM name, it was assigned a forward P/E of roughly 3.7x. Read that multiple literally, and investors are kind of assuming the business earns back today's capital commitment in about four years — and then, implicitly, that the good times stop there. That's what an "honest price tag" looks like once the market has been burned enough times by memory's boom-bust cycle.

## Rationale — Why This Happened

- **New assets lack a shared valuation anchor.** With no history, no comparable set, and no agreed cash-flow model, price is set by narrative and positioning rather than discounted expectations. That vacuum is precisely where early information advantages are worth the most — and where latecomers overpay for a story.
- **Early-stage holder psychology props up fragile prices.** Hopeful holders don't sell into strength; informed players distribute into strength. The result is a market that *looks* stable while ownership quietly transfers from strong hands to weak ones. The correction, when it comes, feels sudden — but the fragility was built in from day one.
- **Corrections are the market's tuition fee for learning cyclicality.** Before the bust, a booming asset is priced as if growth is secular. After it, the same asset is priced as a cycle. Neither extreme is "rational" in isolation — but the second price tag is usually the more honest one, because it has been paid for with real losses.
- **Low multiples encode cyclical distrust, not necessarily value.** A 3.7x forward P/E on a memory giant isn't the market being stupid; it's the market refusing to extrapolate peak earnings. For cyclical businesses, peak earnings × a "cheap" multiple can still be a full price. The multiple is a statement about *where we are in the cycle*, not just about the company.

## Key Takeaways

1. **Mispricing is widest where understanding is thinnest.** New assets, new situations, and obscure instruments offer the biggest gaps between price and value — in both directions. Enter for the opportunity, but budget for the education.
2. **Ask who is distributing and who is holding.** In early-stage frenzies, map the incentives: early informed players have every reason to prop the narrative while exiting. If you can't identify the strong hands, assume you're the weak one.
3. **Use corrections as research catalysts, not just pain.** The post-crash phase is when an asset's true cyclicality becomes visible and measurable. That's when honest price tags get written — and often when the *real* long-term entry points appear.
4. **Translate multiples into implied expectations.** A forward P/E is not just a number; it's a compressed forecast. "3.7x" literally says: the market pays for ~4 years of current earnings and nothing beyond. Asking *why* the market believes that teaches you more than the multiple itself.
5. **Secular stories in cyclical clothing are the most dangerous.** When a cycle is dressed up as a structural growth story ("this time it's different — it's AI-driven"), demand the evidence that distinguishes the two before paying a secular price for a cyclical peak.

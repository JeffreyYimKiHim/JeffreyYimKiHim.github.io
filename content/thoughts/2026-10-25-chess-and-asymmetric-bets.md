# Chess, Missed Opportunities, and the Case for Asymmetric Bets

Hindsight is always clear. I play chess to kill time, but I keep coming back to it not because I win, but because it reminds me of something humbling: even when all the information is completely laid out on the board, the "best thing" to do at this moment can still carry enormous uncertainty — at least for a human with limited mental bandwidth (not so much for an engine).

Chess keeps teaching me a lesson that applies directly to investing. At this very moment, somewhere in a quiet corner of the market, there must be interesting projects and companies that I could rightfully have skin in. But our brains are not wired like massive compute engines engrained in a real-time news feed monitoring the world. Our minds are mortal, limited, bounded — affected by sleep, health, and mood. Arguably the brain was designed to be a poet first: to express some appreciation for the world it sees, not to scan every ticker on earth.

So every day, hundreds of opportunities are missed. This is what makes it so hard to be a good investor: you have to admit that, to a large extent, you *will* be a loser — you will miss stakes in opportunities that, in hindsight, were obvious. And yet you still need the courage to make tangible bets on projects that, on a forward-looking basis, are *expected* to have a good enough outcome. Not certainty — expectation.

Which is why, on reflection, mid-delta convertible bonds have become so interesting to me. They offer an asymmetric payoff: bond-like downside cushioning when the thesis is wrong, equity-like participation when it is right. Given that I have already conceded I cannot see everything, an instrument that forgives partial blindness is probably the best defense one can get.

## Why this matters

Most investing content sells the fantasy of completeness — better screens, faster news, more data, as if the gap between us and full information could be closed. This reflection argues the opposite: the gap is structural, not fixable, and the mature response is to design around it rather than deny it.

That shift in posture has practical consequences. If missed opportunities are guaranteed, then (a) regret is not a signal of failure, (b) the quality bar for the bets you *do* make matters more than the quantity, and (c) payoff structure — how much you make when right versus lose when wrong — deserves at least as much attention as idea selection. The mid-delta convertible is simply the concrete expression of that philosophy: a position sized for a mind that knows it is bounded.

## Takeaways

- **Accept structural incompleteness.** You will miss most good opportunities — everyone does. Measuring yourself against perfect hindsight is a category error; measure yourself against your forward-looking process instead.
- **Bet on expectations, not certainties.** Even with full information (as in chess), the best move is uncertain. In markets, information is never full. Courage is required precisely because the outcome is only ever *expected*, never known.
- **Prioritize payoff asymmetry.** When you cannot eliminate error, choose instruments and structures where being wrong is survivable and being right is well-paid. Mid-delta convertibles are one example; find the structures that fit your own mandate.
- **Design for the human you are.** Health, bandwidth, and attention are real constraints on decision quality. A strategy that requires superhuman monitoring will fail in human hands — build one that works at mortal capacity.

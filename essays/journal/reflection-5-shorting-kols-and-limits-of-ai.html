"Shorting, KOLs, and the Limits of AI — Why 'What NOT to Buy' May Be AI's Best Use"

25 Jul 2026 · Thesis Journal

The Story

Shorting overbought sectors looks attractive upfront. But stop and do the math: the best possible upside on a short is 100% — the asset goes to zero — while the downside is theoretically unlimited. Long positions, by contrast, can be multi-baggers. The asymmetry runs against you from the moment you put the trade on.

So how did "The Short Bear" do it? The legendary short sellers we read about didn't just bet against expensive things; they found fraud, broken models, and catalysts — and they sized, timed, and survived the trade while being early (which, in shorting, is indistinguishable from being wrong until you're right).

Meanwhile, too many KOLs are out there telling you that something "works," when in fact the actual risk and judgment still sit fully with the individual investor. Even with AI, you're limited by information dissemination friction: AI can only summarize what has already been written down, and by the time a thesis is written down well enough for AI to find it, the market has usually priced it.

So no — investing with AI is hardly a reliable game for individuals. At best, AI's highest-value role is probably the negative screen: asking it "what NOT to invest in."

Rationale — Why This Is True

  • Shorting is structurally asymmetric against you. A long can return multiples of capital and loses at most 100%; a short returns at most 100% and can lose multiples of capital. Add borrow costs, squeeze risk, recall risk, and the market's long-run upward drift, and shorting becomes a game where you must be right on the thesis and the timing and the path.
  • Famous short trades were information trades, not valuation trades. The celebrated shorts that made fortunes were built on primary research that revealed something the market structurally could not see — fraudulent accounting, unsustainable structures, mispriced correlation. "It's overbought" is not information; everyone can see the chart. A short thesis without an informational edge is just a donation to the borrow desk.
  • KOL conviction transfers to you, but their risk does not. A KOL who is wrong loses followers; a follower who copies them loses capital. Every "this works" claim silently skips the position sizing, entry timing, exit discipline, and the portfolio context that made it work for them. Judgment cannot be copy-traded.
  • AI inherits the internet's latency. Large models are trained and grounded on published text. As I learned in the semiconductor trade, the people who profited most knew 1–2 months before the press did. AI therefore structurally cannot close the information gap that matters most in fast trades — it can only make the published layer cheaper to read.
  • Negative screening is where latency matters least. "What NOT to invest in" — accounting red flags, crowded positioning, unsustainable capital structures, governance problems — lives in documents that already exist and change slowly. That is exactly the layer AI reads well. Using AI as a filter rather than an oracle plays to its strength and sidesteps its latency.

Key Takeaways

  1. Do the asymmetry math before shorting anything. Cap your enthusiasm: best case +100%, worst case uncapped, plus borrow costs and squeeze risk. If you still want the trade, demand an informational edge, a catalyst, and a predefined exit — not just a high valuation.
  2. Separate the KOL's payoff from yours. Before acting on any "this works" claim, ask what the promoter loses if wrong. If the answer is "nothing," their conviction is free to them and expensive to you.
  3. Use AI as a filter, not an oracle. Its comparative advantage is digesting the published record: filings, red flags, inconsistencies, crowded narratives. Ask it "what should I avoid and why" far more often than "what should I buy."
  4. Accept that the last mile of alpha is analog. The 1–2 month information lead that actually pays comes from primary research, industry contacts, and original work — things that are, by definition, not yet written down. AI compresses research time; it cannot replace information advantage.
  5. Keep the risk and the judgment in-house. Tools, KOLs, and AI can inform a decision, but the capital at risk is yours. Any process where the decision-maker bears no risk for being wrong will eventually transfer wealth away from you.

This is a personal investment reflection, not investment advice.

"Reflection 1: Information Asymmetry Is a Cruel Thing — Lessons from the Semiconductor Supercycle"

25 Oct 2026 · Thesis Journal

The Story

Information asymmetry is a cruel thing.

The first time I learned about Kioxia and SanDisk was during my days as a junior trader, when I was helping to size trades. Without anyone asking for it, I took the extra step of digging into the companies myself — and that's when I learned about their chips business. This was in 2H 2025, when the market was still in a full "gold rush" frenzy. And trust me: none of the mainstream media or finance YouTubers were talking about semiconductors at all. At least, that's what my feeds showed me.

What followed over the next 6–8 months was a rally faster than anything Bitcoin ever achieved. Kioxia rallied roughly 30x within six months, and SanDisk delivered a similar magnitude of returns.

So why am I not financially free? For starters, I stayed compliant and true to the 30-day holding period. Every time I sized a trade, I effectively had to foresee what would happen after those 30 days — and that depended entirely on my grasp of the latest developments and the authenticity of every tweet coming from the "Serenity gang," who posted every day, every hour. With hindsight, those who trusted Situational Awareness and Serenity could either be set free or burnt down to their very last cent — it all depended on when in the rally they started listening to these KOLs.

A crowded trade doesn't appear overnight. It emerges from information disseminating over time, and then it draws in waves of hedge funds setting up heavily skewed options positions — which eventually lead to massive unwinds that shove the market one-sidedly. Picture large block put purchases for downside protection, forcing dealers to hedge their delta frantically; on a crowded trade, that hedging cascade becomes a landslide. To make things worse, margin balances climbed like never before, which attracted leveraged ETFs into the market. By June, every single ingredient for a massive blowup had been assembled.

And yet — the juiciest capital returns often come right before the market blows off its top. I knew that, so I couldn't help sending in more ETF orders near the top. ETFs were already the most flexible instrument in my arsenal, since going in and out required no extra approval.

I was also among the early-stage investors in DRAM in April 2026. By October 2026 — barely six months later — it had set the world record as the fastest-growing ETF, while simultaneously delivering a deeply disappointing performance since 2H 2026. It's an eerie pattern: the more explosive the stock market, the more attention it draws — and that attention eventually pulls in marginal industry players rolling out business plans to contribute production, even when they're several generations behind. (CXMT, for those wondering who I'm referring to.)

That was the ups and downs of my semiconductor trade. And here's the funny part: even with AI, you're only fed what's already written on the internet. Many of those who made it out alive knew what was coming one to two months before mainstream media hit the press.

Rationale — Why This Happened

  • Information flows in waves, not all at once. Early knowledge of a supply bottleneck (NAND/DRAM capacity, in this case) sits with industry participants and the few investors who do primary research. Financial media, YouTube, and AI tools all operate on published information — by construction, they are the last hop in the chain. Whoever relies on them is trading on the oldest information in the market.
  • Crowded trades become mechanically unstable. Once a thesis is consensus, positioning itself becomes the risk. Heavy put buying for "protection" forces dealers short delta, and their hedging amplifies every down move. Add record margin debt and leveraged ETFs (which must buy more on up days and sell more on down days), and you have a market that is structurally programmed to overshoot in both directions.
  • Bottleneck supercycles carry the seeds of their own destruction. Explosive pricing is a signal to every marginal producer on the planet. Even technologically lagging players (CXMT in DRAM) will add capacity once prices are high enough to make their economics work. Supply response is slower than the stock rally, but it always arrives — which is why "bottleneck-led explosive revenue growth" trades have a shelf life.

Key Takeaways

  1. For "bottleneck → explosive revenue growth" ideas, map the information chain before the valuation. Ask: who knows this first, who knows it now, and who will learn it last? If your source is mainstream media or an AI summary, you are likely the exit liquidity.
  2. Compliance constraints are a feature, not just a cost. A forced 30-day holding period turned my thesis into a forward-looking exercise. Constraints forced discipline; respect yours rather than engineering around them.
  3. Watch for the blowup ingredients, not just the price action: crowded positioning, skewed options flows, record margin balances, and leveraged-ETF inflows. When all four coexist, the trade's risk/reward has inverted — even if the story is still intact.
  4. The best returns and the worst entry points are often neighbors. "The juiciest returns come right before the top" is exactly why the top is the top. If you must participate late, size it as a momentum trade, not an investment.
  5. Monitor the supply response as your exit signal. When second-tier producers announce capacity, the supercycle clock is ticking. In commodity-like tech (memory especially), new supply — not demand disappointment — is usually what ends the party.

This is a personal investment reflection, not investment advice. Figures recounted here are from my own trading experience and memory.

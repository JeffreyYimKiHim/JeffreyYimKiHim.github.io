"The Return of Fundamental Research — When the Tide Goes Out"

1 Oct 2026 · Thesis Journal

The Story

Traditional fundamental research was declared useless during the last chips rally cycle — and honestly, for a while, it was. When a stock can rally 30x on a bottleneck story, discounted cash flow models feel like bringing a spreadsheet to a knife fight.

But fundamentals have recently regained their importance. Yields have gone up, and now the tide is truly out: every bit of revenue gets scrutinized — not just for the next five years, but for the sustainability of the business over the next ten, even twenty years.

Why the longer lens? Because too many companies issued debt stretching into the 10–20 year horizon during the cheap-money era. When a company has bondholders due in 2040, someone has to believe the business model survives to 2040. That question cannot be answered with momentum, narratives, or KOL tweets. It can only be answered with old-fashioned fundamental research.

Rationale — Why This Happened

  • The discount rate is the gravity of valuation. When yields are near zero, distant cash flows are worth almost as much as near ones, so stories about far-future dominance can justify almost any price. When yields rise, gravity returns: far-future cash flows shrink in present value, and only businesses with durable, defensible economics retain their valuations.
  • Duration cuts both ways — equity and debt. High-multiple growth equities are long-duration assets: their value sits far in the future, so rising rates hit them hardest. Meanwhile, companies that issued 10–20 year debt in the easy-money era have locked in obligations whose repayment depends on the business surviving intact for decades. Rising yields force the market to actually check whether it will.
  • Liquidity regimes decide which style of analysis "works." In flush-liquidity regimes, capital chases scarcity and momentum; fundamentals lag prices. In tightening regimes, capital demands proof of durability; fundamentals lead prices. The same analyst, with the same skills, is "useless" in one regime and indispensable in the other — which is why declaring any research method permanently dead is a regime error, not an insight.
  • Debt maturity walls turn storytelling into audit. When debt comes due, refinancing is a verdict. A company rolling bonds at higher rates needs lenders to underwrite its long-term survival — and credit underwriters are the most conservative fundamental analysts in the market. Equity investors are now, willy-nilly, reading the same questions.

Key Takeaways

  1. Match your analytical toolkit to the liquidity regime. When yields fall and liquidity flushes, narrative and momentum dominate; when yields rise, durability and cash flow dominate. The skill is recognizing the regime change early, not defending one toolkit forever.
  2. Let the debt maturity schedule set your research horizon. A company with bonds due in 15+ years has pre-committed to being underwritten on long-run survival. Its equity is, in effect, a claim behind that promise — so analyze it with the same 10–20 year lens its creditors will use.
  3. "Fundamentals are dead" is always a cyclical statement. Every era of declared obsolescence (1999, 2021, the chips rally) has ended with fundamentals returning to the throne, usually violently. Treat obituaries for fundamental research as a sentiment indicator, not a fact.
  4. Rising yields are a filter, not just a headwind. They don't punish all companies equally — they punish fragile ones and reveal durable ones. For the prepared investor, the tide going out is when the best research actually gets paid.

This is a personal investment reflection, not investment advice.
